OET referral letter: the structure that scores (Medicine)

The referral letter is the single most common OET Medicine writing task you'll be asked to produce, and it's also the one where structure does most of the work. Two candidates can have exactly the same clinical knowledge in front of them — yet one scores 350 and the other 290. The difference is almost never vocabulary or grammar; it is whether the letter is built on a structure the assessor can follow in the first two lines, and kept disciplined enough to score cleanly across all six criteria.

The task stimulus assigns everything

Before you write a single sentence, read the task stimulus as an instruction from another clinician. It tells you two things that decide the whole shape of your letter: who it goes to and why it's being sent. Those two facts define the genre.

  • A referral goes to a specialist or senior colleague asking them to take over or advise on part of a patient's care — you are handing something forward.
  • A discharge letter goes back to the referring GP once the episode ends — you are reporting what happened and what continues.
  • An urgent letter is defined by its purpose: it needs a specific action or decision quickly, not merely a summary.

The trap most candidates fall into is inferring urgency from the case notes. A dramatic presentation — chest pain, acute confusion, a collapsing haemoglobin — does not make the letter urgent. Urgency is assigned by the task: if it says "urgent" or asks for a decision within a timeframe, you write and address it as urgent; if it doesn't, you don't manufacture urgency from how alarming the case sounds. The recipient and the purpose come from the stimulus, not from your reading of the drama.

The four-movement structure

A scoring referral letter moves through four distinct sections, one movement per paragraph. Get this order right and the six criteria largely take care of themselves, because each movement is doing a job the rubric rewards.

  1. Opening purpose lines. Name the patient and state exactly what you want in the first two or three lines — "I am referring Mr X, a 68-year-old with..." followed by the reason. This is where Purpose and Genre & Style are won or lost; the request must be unmistakable before the reader reaches line four.
  2. Background / current state. The relevant history and present condition — but only what the task notes point to, not a full social and family history pulled in out of habit.
  3. Management to date. What has already been done and how the patient has responded. This shows the specialist where things stand before they take over.
  4. The specific request. Close by spelling out what you need from them — an assessment, a decision, a procedure, advice on fitness to drive — so the letter lands as a genuine handover rather than a report that simply ends.

When these four movements are in place and each occupies its own paragraph, Organisation & Layout reads as effortless: the assessor never has to hunt for the purpose or the request because they sit where they always should.

Selection is the scoring lever

The single biggest mark-leak in referral letters is not what's missing — it's what shouldn't be there. Candidates routinely expand on details that the task notes never justify, simply to look thorough. A lengthy social history, a full medication list repeated verbatim from the notes, or a paragraph of background about an unrelated comorbidity all read as padding.

The discipline is this: expand only what the stimulus points to, and cut everything else. If a case note doesn't advance the reason for referral or the management decision you're asking for, it fails Content — details that don't serve the letter's purpose aren't thoroughness, they're dead weight — and it also costs marks under Conciseness even though it looks helpful. That is why word count is not a side issue — it's the symptom of selection. Hold hard to 180–200 words; when you're tempted to add "just one more detail," that instinct is usually the leak itself.

Self-check before you submit

Use these five questions as your final pass. If any answer isn't a clear yes, fix it before time runs out — you cannot recover a buried purpose line once the letter is done.

  • Is the purpose unmistakable in the first two lines? The recipient should know who the patient is and what you want within seconds.
  • Does every sentence earn its place? Could any paragraph be cut without losing what the task asked for? If so, cut it.
  • Is the request specific? Not "please review," but exactly what you need them to do and, where relevant, why it's urgent.
  • Is the register clinician-to-clinician? One professional to another — "for endoscopic assessment" rather than a lay description of what an endoscopy is.
  • Are you within 180–200 words? If you're well past it, the excess is almost always unselected detail, not thoroughness.
