OET SPEAKING · SOLO PRACTICE

OET speaking practice alone: a routine that actually transfers to test day

Most OET candidates' speaking practice stalls because they think they need a partner. You don't. The role-play is a structured conversation — and structure is something you can rehearse alone, every day, in five-minute blocks.

Why solo practice works for the speaking sub-test

The speaking sub-test is two five-minute role-plays. In each one you are the healthcare professional; the interlocutor plays a patient or a relative. You get a few minutes to read the role-play card, then you open the conversation. The marks come from clinical communication behaviour — how you open, how you structure, how you question, how you close — not from medical knowledge and not from small-talk fluency. All of that behaviour is trainable without a partner, because you are training your own half of the conversation.

The 5-minute role-play frame (memorise this)

  1. Warm open (10–15 seconds). Greet, introduce yourself and your role, check the patient's name. "Good morning, I'm one of the nurses at the clinic — you must be Mr Rao?"
  2. Agree the agenda. Say why you're here and what you'd like to cover. This single move separates a 350+ performance from a rambling one: the interlocutor knows where you're going, and so do you.
  3. Open questions before closed. Start with "Tell me about…" / "How have the… been?" Use closed questions only to pin down specifics later.
  4. Empathise and explain, don't lecture. Acknowledge concerns before correcting them. Explain in plain language; check understanding as you go.
  5. Summarise and close with a check. "So just to sum up — … Is there anything else you'd like to ask?" Candidates who skip this look like they ran out of ideas, even when the content was fine.

The solo routine (20 minutes a day)

  • One timed role-play, out loud, standing up (5 min). Use any scenario you can write in two lines: a worried parent, a patient refusing medication, a relative asking about discharge. Write the card like the exam does: your role / the patient's role / what you need to cover.
  • Record it, then listen once — for structure only. Did you set the agenda? Open questions first? Summarise? Don't judge your accent; judge the frame.
  • Second attempt of the same scenario with one fix. Changing one behaviour per rep is how the frame becomes automatic.
  • Once a week, a harder variant: an angry or distressed patient, or a relative who keeps interrupting. Difficulty is a skill dimension of its own.

Where an AI role-play partner fits

A partner is ideal but rare; a script read aloud is safe but never pushes back. The gap between them is what AI is good for: a patient who answers unpredictably, goes off-script, or gets difficult on cue — so your frame gets tested, not just recited.
